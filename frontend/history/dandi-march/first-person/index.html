INDIA · APRIL 1930

One act, many marches

People elsewhere could defy the same law. In the Madras Presidency, Rajaji led a separate march.

Separate routes · not to scale

Why it spread

Everyone needed salt. By openly breaking the law without violence, people could turn an everyday need into a protest. In Tamil Nadu, people along Rajaji’s route welcomed marchers and offered support.

Rajaji left Tiruchirappalli on 13 April and arrived at Vedaranyam on 28 April. Like at Dandi, arrival and the salt act were separate events.

Before and after the march
  • 1929: After opposition to the Simon Commission, the Congress at Lahore declared complete independence (Purna Swaraj) its goal.
  • 1930: The Salt March became part of the wider Civil Disobedience Movement. Many people joined protests and many were arrested.
  • 1931–32: The Gandhi–Irwin Pact paused the campaign. Gandhi attended the Second Round Table Conference. After talks failed to settle the issue, Civil Disobedience resumed in 1932.

Archive and reconstruction

The opening uses genuine 1930 Salt March footage from the GandhiServe/Wikimedia Commons compilation, listed there as public domain in India and the US. The score and footsteps are modern. The edit does not identify a precise filming location.

After the cut, you are an imagined local child. The companion, and the photographic-style reconstructions are imagined. The coastal salt image is an illustration, not an archival photograph of Gandhi. Gandhi does not speak. No synthetic dialogue plays. Aslali was the march’s first night halt. Gandhi’s opposition to the salt tax included its burden on poor families.
